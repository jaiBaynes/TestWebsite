*Myth 1.5: Hercules at Delphi

Jupiter had charged Zeus with upholding Fate. Zeus at once...got someone else to do it. But who could accomplish such a monumental task? Who else but the rising star: Hercules, the son of Zeus! The hero never failed to destroy anything his father pointed him at. Mission after mission, labor after labor, the enemies of Zeus fell. In a few short years, one man had accomplished more than all the other gods in Zeus's employ. There were whispers that he might be worthy of godhood himself...

![Hercules by bentejam](/images/characters/hercules.png)


After his latest victory over Zeus's enemies, Hercules arrived victorious in Delphi, the city of Apollo. The metropolis was built around the sun god’s grand temple, for he was also the god of music, healing, legislation, and—most importantly—prophecy. The more divine domains, the more prestigious the god; Apollo’s popularity was second only to that of his heavenly father, Zeus. The Thunderer, a prolific philanderer, had countless sons, but in Delphi there was but one son of Zeus, and it was Apollo.
That was why the priests of Apollo grew outraged when Hercules’s arrival to their cultic center was met with parades and celebration. This wasn’t his first time in Delphi, but with each return, adorned by yet more exploits, his fame only grew and grew. This was his most triumphant moment yet: Hercules was returning victorious after slaying the wicked Hydra.
To honor the momentous occasion, he was ushered in upon a gilded chariot: the Hercules collab brand with Hermes’s Cult of Transportation. As he passed through the streets in his iconic lion hood, wielding his giant club, a great crowd assembled, hailing him as god’s true son.
People from all walks of life came to see him. Men shouted and cheered for him while their wives peered out from within their homes. The highest noblemen dressed in their finest to greet him. Even the lowest Scythian and demi-human slaves, fear of the lash be damned, abandoned their toil to watch the hero’s entrance. The highest and lowest of Graia’s hierarchies felt united in their love of this universal icon: Hercules!
The hero lived for this; the adoration had him absolutely glowing. The praise of worshippers, the glory of battle, and the immortality of story and song were the bedrock of Graia’s heroes, after all.
But this was Apollo’s city; Hercules being shown such favoritism was unheard of. Though both men were Zeus’s sons, only Apollo had a divine mother to match. Hercules was born of a mortal woman; the way in which he was idolized was an insult against Graia’s strict social hierarchies. Not to mention the infidelity of the Delphians worshipping anyone above their Lord and Master.
After all, cults were not just religious organizations—they were also moneymaking institutions. The gods’ lavish lifestyles wouldn’t pay for themselves; cults extracted tribute from their followers to pay for those huge bills. A loss in status was a loss in revenue, and that could not be allowed.
When Zeus’s son arrived at his brother's temple, the Delphic priesthood was less loving than the public. The smug hero entered the temple as if he owned the place, ready for his private audience with Apollo’s mortal prophet. He offered a sacrificial cow to his father, Zeus, and not his host god brother.
The Oracle’s prim and proper temple was disgusted to see the blood-soaked, lion-wearing barbarian dragging mud into its pristine halls. This was who dared to lead the people away from the radiant one?
“You’re a different Oracle than usual. Where’s the hot chick?” Hercules noted.  
“The Pythia priestess is out of town giving a keynote speech at this year’s prophecy convention,” the old priest explained as he sat on the seat of prophecy: Apollo’s tripod. 
“Ha! Ironically, she must not’ve seen me coming.” Hercules laughed and kicked his feet up on the table between the seer and himself. 
The old man was not amused. “I am a veteran prophet of my radiant Lord and Master, Apollo, first son of Zeus. I shall perform your reading in the Pythia’s stead,” he groaned.
“Great. My father told me I was fated to come here for a purpose. So, let’s hear it.” Hercules grinned, making the cult leader practically burst a blood vessel.
“Beware, young brute. Your impiety begs for punishment. You are destined to become a slave to your better—and only then find fulfilment. Maybe then you shall learn proper manners!” the man vindictively declared.
Hercules was given pause. He laughed. Then he saw that the man was glaring at him. “You’re serious?”
“Quite. Serious offenses deserve serious responses. Now leave.”
“No no no, try again. Let’s see if I get a better one.”
“That isn’t how this works, you simpleton! Cease blaspheming against the gods! Go!”
“I was sent here by my father, Zeus!” Hercules stomped. “He is god of storms, kings—and hospitality! You have been most inhospitable to me! Disrespecting me is disrespecting him!” 
“Silence! In Delphi, Zeus’s son is Lord Apollo! Here, you are a nobody!”
Through the hail of insults and mockery, that one straw broke the camel’s back. Hercules’s demigod Power flared out in a terrifying inferno. The brute at once threw the old man away and seized the sacred tripod of Apollo: the seat of prophecy.
A group of the cult’s security rounded the corner, chasing Hercules down with a shout. They were sent flying through the temple walls and landed on the road outside. A crowd gathered to see the great brute, Hercules, pummeling an entire small army of Apollo’s servants as they tried and failed to recover their master’s property.
With the superhuman strength Power gave him, he triumphed over these ordinary men. Before long, Hercules stood above the defeated acolytes without a scratch on him. He’d beaten them all with his bare hands. Whether they lived or died was not his concern. The son of Zeus prepared to walk out with Apollo’s tripod.
"Tiny mortal, what a displeasure… Where do you think you're going with my treasure?" a voice demanded from above, compelling the acolytes to sing along. 
Hercules turned to find that sun-kissed Apollo himself had descended to personally attend to the troublemaker.

![Apollo, the radiant sun god by bentejam](/images/characters/apollo.png)


All who bore witness to this fell prostrate and worshipped him, offering not only praise, but pleas to be forgiven and spared. In Graia, the ruling gods were both loved and feared. 
“A seat both fine and divine; I assume that you know…it is mine.” Apollo, the sun god landed and pointed at the tripod in Hercules’s hand.
Hercules paused for a moment. He’d worked out much of his destructive anger, but now, he was feeling a sense of immature spite. 
“This yours? Come take it then…bro.” Hercules smirked. 
The crowd gathered panicked; he was going to get them all killed. 
Apollo just sighed. At once, Hercules’s entire body burst into flames. Only the tripod he held was divinely spared.  
“A fool deluded by fame, now engulfed in his optimate’s flame. A god he defied, his last chance denied, and now… he has died.” Apollo strummed his harp.
However, the flames consuming Hercules suddenly extinguised, revealing that the hero was just fine inside his aura of Power 
“That’s the best that the sun god can manage?” Hercules chuckled while stroking his beard.
Apollo was not amused. “By Fate, you’ve been spared from my burning.” The god plucked his lyre more aggressively, “Now hurry and return what I’m yearning…”
“Bad rhyme.” Hercules chuckled.
“Return—”
“Make me,” Hercules continued, egging him on like a grade schooler.
The golden-armed god cast fire all over Hercules again. However, the hero put it out with the wave of his hand. Suddenly, he noticed that the divinity had swiped the tripod back.
“Delphi knells to suffer this clown. It’s time you get out of my town.” Apollo grabbed Hercules by his lionskin cloak and moved to throw him with his godly strength.
However, all were stunned when Apollo failed to even budge Hercules. The sun god pulled and pulled, but when the greatest of all heroes didn’t want to be moved…he wasn’t. 
“Get out of town? Who exactly is going to make me?” Hercules asked as he looked down at his brother.
Apollo grew annoyed at these antics. “Listen here—” 
Mid-sentence, Hercules swung his club and shattered the tripod. And that was that. 
In the next moment, an explosion of lightning and fire destroyed much of the temple. Panic spread as the temple servants and the citizens of Delphi fled from the battle of Zeus’s sons. As they clashed, they lit up all of Delphi and put the fear of Zeus in all the poor souls caught in their wake.
