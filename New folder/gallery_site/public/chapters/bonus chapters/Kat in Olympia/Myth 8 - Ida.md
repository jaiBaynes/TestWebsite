*Myth ?.516: Ida

Stavroula, a Cretan immigrant to Olympia, got up early to set up for weaving club in her house. Weaving was a key feminine activity in Graia, though it was in many ways associated with “older women.” One of the many changes Stavroula had to get used to in this new land was finding that in Graia, at 40 years old, she was considered an “old woman.”
Graia, a land without modern medicine, had such an insanely high infant mortality rate that the average life expectancy was 30 years old. Even women who survived the dangers of childhood faced the danger of childbirth: an equally precarious state in Graia.
For that and a cavalcade of many other reasons, Stavroula led various women’s groups created in the past. On top of it being nice to have a safe third space for women to meet and socialize, these communities were also essential for creating networks to counter stalking, domestic violence and other overlooked female-facing problems.
Stavroula was disappointed to find even Cretan men had fallen and become threats her groups needed to protect against. She knew them before Crete’s fall; they were good men. All that time were they really a few bad days away from becoming the kind of filth they detested in Graia? Or was it this wicked empire itself which corrupted them?
She stood ready to face this rising threat yet her reputation as pillar of the community was being challenged by a new player: Pikrosa the wife of Eupatridus. The wealthy old woman lived in the slums, but her luxurious lifestyle confined her to the solitary segregated life expected of upper-class women. Until recently she didn’t leave the house.
Pikrosa often mentioned how nice it was to have company again. Her life before ascending to the luxurious wife of a tyrant was full of suffering… but not without some joy. In her old age, she seemed to regret leaving behind her friends to live isolated in a palace. Perhaps she genuinely regretted selfishly leaving them all to rot. Hoping to make up for lost time, she was always enthusiastic to engage with the women of the club.
Pikrosa seemed excited to rejoin the more communal existence that lower class life necessitated for women. There was no “staying confined in the gynaeceum” when poverty required all hands-on deck. She assigned bodyguards to protect threatened women and charitably distributed essentials at the weekly weaving meetings (including expensive new looms for the club).
The help was greatly appreciated, but Stavroula couldn’t help but be skeptical of her motives. The rich and powerful rarely did anything altruistically. Even if she did, once she got bored of this pet project it was likely her charity would dry up. Stavroula needed to stay on guard at all times of Pikrosa… and her strange friend.
Rather than weaving in isolation as Graia expected, Stavroula’s weaving club was in the process of creating a group project. Inspired by her native Cretan culture, literal patchworks of different people’s artwork like this were essential to create communal sunshades and beat the heat on her tropical island home.
The non-Cretan members of the weaving club quickly adjusted and created sections of the shade using traditional Graiac patterns to integrate with the Cretan ones. More interesting than the different colors of different cities, Pikrosa noted her amazement that Cretan people were themselves a very mixed tapestry.
Matching different patterns and styles from Crete’s women were the notably various skin tones and facial features that could be found on the women all claiming Cretan origins. What Pikrosa would identify as Phoenicians, Graiacs, Anatolians, Aegyptians, Aethiopians and more all identified themselves under the banner of Cretan.
“The style of Minoa is quite fascinating.” Pikrosa smiled.
“Yes, Cretan crafts are second to none.” Stavroula, the proud Cretan subtly corrected her.
Pikrosa paused, realizing her mistake. “Minoa” was the colonized name Graia assigned to the Cretan people after Zeus’ son Minos who was the first to colonize the island. They made no secret of their hatred for the label. They were the proud people of Melisseus’ Crete not Minos’ Minoa.
“I’m sorry. I’ll admit I don’t know much about Crete.” Pikrosa apologized, noting the sudden tension in the room.
“Well, what would you like to learn?” Kat took that as an invitation to talk about Crete.
She spoke with much confidence about the Cretan people despite not being one herself. Most alarming was the accuracy and intimate familiarity she held with the island, its people and its history despite never having been there.
After handing out the baked goods she and Pikrosa brought to these weekly meetings for all members (and the children who stayed with Stavroula), Kat sat down and eagerly discussed Crete: the “Fruit Basket of the world.”
She eagerly explained the history of these sunshades, first invented by the wife of Crete’s original pre-Graiac ruler in a weaving club much like this one. Despite her station, she and her husband represented rather than ruled. They dwelt among the common people and took their plights into account as they governed. This first Queen knew the importance of hearing the plight of Crete’s women and ensuring their needs were met. Complaints about summer heat in the market were met with this response: sunshades woven by members of the community to be hung above the marketplace.
“It’s a practice that persisted even after the colonization by Minos and exploded in popularity after Crete’s liberation by the Cretan heroes Hydra and Melissa.” Kat finished her explanation.
The Cretan women at the meeting were pleased by Kat’s explanation. However, Pikrosa was a bit uncomfortable. As a Graiac, she learned that the terrorist Hydra and tyrant Melissa were violent villains who overthrew the rightful King Minos who was chosen by Zeus and gave the city over to the dreaded “mob rule.” However, Pikrosa read the room. The Cretans hated Minos and loved Hydra and Melissa as “revolutionary figures.”
Kat told her to be open-minded and so she came to learn about the perspective of these alien peoples. To her relief, none of them directly pledged loyalty to that dreaded political poison which governed Crete before its “re-liberation” by reconquest 4 years ago. Though many of them made clear they were upset about Graia again conquering their home (which had led to their displacement) they were very careful to not say any praise about the peculiar system of government which they held beforehand: democracy.
They were wise to Pikrosa’s Graiac mentality and reactions. They could not hold back their disgust for their enemies but were very-tightly lipped about any praise for their homeland and its proud egalitarian, democratic tradition. The only one who fearlessly discussed the topic was Kat.
Though she never used any of the demonized terms like “democracy” which might set off alarm bells with Pikrosa, Kat was quick to sing the praises of many aspects of Cretan governance. That girl was very mysterious, her knowledge of not just Crete but various other democratic cities extended beyond the theoretical and into the practical. Stavroula noticed many of her programs were not without precedent: they nearly all came from reformers and revolutionaries in foreign lands.
The free bread program was almost wholesale ripped from the Cretan city of Dreros’ dole system which became a model for revitalization across the rest of the island. In that program she was sure to employ many Cretan bakers to produce island pastries to both give Cretans a sense of home while also winning over new Graiac fans. The various construction and infrastructure plans reflected the capital Cretan city Knossos’ ambitious building programs. Cretan builders were finally able to again apply their skills and earn an honest living instead of begging.
Obviously, these borrowed policies did not only benefit the slums’ Cretan residents. Graia’s oppressed poor were quite happy with democratic governance as long as Kat didn’t tell them it was democratic in origin. What they didn’t know couldn’t hurt them. But then… what did she know?
Graiacs could only think of Crete monolithically: for different cities coexisting as equals instead of colonizer and colonized was an alien concept. In their view Cretans were all just one big city on an island. Yet, Kat was clearly familiar with Cretan cities like Dreros, Knossos, Gortyn and others. She could tell members of the weaving club’s hometown by the minutiae of their dialect and fashion. As impressive and nice as it was to meet one who understood them, Stavroula and her friends were somewhat unnerved by just how much this girl knew.
“In Crete, the ruler isn’t called a king, they’re a Kosmos. It’s a yearly position where the most popular person enacts their vision for the city.” Kat described the position while evading terms like elect, vote or democracy.
“What happens after the year do they kill them?” Pikrosa still struggled to wrap her head around the idea.
“That’s old anti-Cretan propaganda. When a Kosmos’ term was up, they would voluntarily step down. They couldn’t hold office for the next ten years.” Kat noted.
“He would voluntarily step down?” Pikrosa was baffled by such a system of power.
“They would. Unlike king, which is a masculine title, the Kosmos is a gender-neutral role because anyone could earn that position. Right?” Kat smiled.
The room went dead silent as many of the women feigned ignorance. And there it was. Stavroula could only come to one conclusion: she was a spy working for Zeus’ Judges. After years of living in Crete constantly bombarded with Judge psy-ops and clandestine attack, perpetual skepticism was the order of the day. Stavroula felt justified in watching Kat and Pikrosa so closely. Kat’s programs did a good job of exposing various prominent Cretans; was she here to help the judges hunt down those of Crete’s political leaders who had gone into hiding?
“Given the fate of our island…” Stavroula spoke up, “many of us do not feel comfortable discussing politics. I suggest you avoid the topic around our home’s survivors. There is no reason to take such interest in what can be painful to discuss.”
“Fair. Many former Kosmos were killed after the war. Given how Cretan politics worked many people probably had personal connections to those who were killed. It was that tightly nit sense of community that caused the purges to go the way they did.” Kat began.
Everyone else was confused by her words in different ways.
“The Graiac army made the same assumption Pikrosa did: hunting down elderly men as the assumed rulers. That was why they were outsmarted by the brilliant and brave sacrifice of the older generation.” Kat’s words gave the others pause, “Before the army arrived, Crete’s government removed all young and female leaders to save the young generation. Elders took their role and died in their place when the purge began. Because of their sacrifice all the women and young people who were in office could escape with their lives.”
“…where did you hear such strange conspiracy theories?” Stavroula tensed up.
“I was keeping track of Cretan officials, and they were not those honored in the funeral after the purge. This was the conclusion I came to. When Graia executed the leader of Crete it was Stavros instead of you, Ida.” Kat said.
Stavroula froze. Pikrosa looked around in confusion; there was no “Ida” in their weaving group.
“Kosmos-emeritus Ida. That is your real name, isn’t it?” Kat smiled sweetly and paralyzed Stavroula with terror, “Stavros was your mentor. When the Graiac coalition army invaded he claimed to be the ruler of Crete and died in your stead. You took the feminine version of his name to honor him after death, didn’t you?”
The other women looked at Stavroula, horrified she had been exposed.
“You came here after learning a great number of Cretan war orphans had become stranded in Olympia’s slums. Rumors of it being a trap for you and your allies be damned.” Kat noted, looking to the scattered evidence of various orphans Stavroula had taken in.
“I see, you’re with the Judges. I came here to help my people living in exile, but I am a failure. Your trap was quite successful; I arrived, accomplished nothing and now die.”
“Accomplished nothing!?” Kat cried, “No way! You’re an icon! Even stripped of all your powers and hiding in the shadows, you rallied people, broke boundaries and networked your way to make the most of your situation. People around this city; some people in this very room owe you their lives!”
Stavroula was stunned by Kat’s vigorous praise.
“Don’t diminish what you did back on Crete, first as Kosmos and then your other positions as you honored the constitution and did not run for Kosmos again until 10 years after your first term. We both know you probably could have won if you did; that’s how much people trusted you! Here in Olympia, your arrival filled the Cretan community with hope. Even now people trust you and I trust you.”
“But why should I trust you?” Stavroula demanded.
“…I trust you because a dear friend of mine spoke very highly of you. She constantly recommended you as an inspiration for me. A lot of what I’ve done in Olympia… is based on what I think you would have done if you had the privileges I do. It’s a bit awkward but you can say I’m a huge fan by proxy.”
Stavroula was visibly confused.
“My penpal shadowed you for her careers day project and gave me all the details. I’ll admit it has been somewhat surreal meeting you in person.”
“The councilor of a Tyrant is impressed by me?” Stavroula laughed.
“I trust you.” Pikrosa, the wife of that tyrant stunned the room, “I’m not sure I fully understand what is happening… but if Kat says you can do right by the women of these slums, I would be happy to introduce you to my husband.”
“You’re serious?” Stavroula laughed.
“We aren’t with the Judges. We don’t want to hurt you, we want to help… and get your help in turn. You will be given power on par with a tyrant and everything you need to apply your governing experience to improve the lives of the people.” Kat said to everyone’s shock, “We gathered all the most prominent women I could get my hands on for just this reason. We’re all friends here. Now we can support each other. We have big plans.”
“I’ll admit you’ve caught my interest.”
“We’d be glad to have you on board, Stavroula. And remember everybody. What happens in weaving club stays in weaving club.’” Kat smiled.

-	The group sunshade tapestry is inspired by: https://www.instagram.com/p/DKu0Dj-y-mp/?igsh=MTllbGl1azVldWp4Zw== 
-	The diversity of Crete’s population in the story is based on possible historical fact. While not conclusively known, there is various evidence 1: (Cretans depicting themselves with various noticeably different skin tones in their own artwork) 2: (Crete’s position in the Mediterranean at an intersection of Europe, Asia and Africa meaning people there might have ancestry from any of those backgrounds)
-	In the story, Crete being known as the “Fruit basket of the world” is based on Cube historically being called the “Sugar bowl of the world.” In the story the title was earned because of Crete’s geopolitical positioning and connections with diverse cultures across the Mediterranean, allowing them access to various exotic fruits and vegetables from Europe, Asia and Africa, all thriving on the island.
-	This setting being based on ancient Greece yet being anti-democratic is a reference to the fact that in the age of Greek mythology Greece was still a collection of monarchies. Democracy would not gain wide popularity in Greece until the 6th century BC. Even when it did, many slavery defending elitists like Plato held anti-democratic sentiments.
-	Dreros is a real city on the island of Crete famous for the “Dreros Inscription” one of the oldest (if not the oldest) democratic constitutions in the world. It replaced the position of king with the position of “Kosmos” a yearly elected office similar to modern presidents and prime ministers.
-	In this story, Crete’s high status of women is based on real life history: Cretan women held far more authority and prestige than their mainland Greek counterparts.
-	Ida is the name of the highest mountain on the island of Crete.
-	Kosmos-emeritus is a position based on the role emeritus which according to the Oxford dictionary means: (of the former holder of an office, especially a college professor) having retired but allowed to retain their title as an honor.
-	Kat trivia: Ida has actually seen something woven by Kat back in Crete but never realized who sent it.
