*Myth ?.510: Bake Sale

PART 1
The slums outside Olympia were a dreadful place for those who failed the game of Mercantilism and ran out of money. Kept away from the enclosed wealth and resources of the big city, they subsisted off scraps with the barest of necessities (if they had even that). The unpaved dirt roads were lined by tightly packed tents and poorly made scrap sheds. Even Kasious’ own bakery was little more than a shed-house with an extra a window to take customers and serve their loaves.
Even with all the skilled craftsmen who were thrown to the slums, without resources or funds they could hardly produce or build better than that. Ironically the main source of furnishment and decoration was the discarded refuse from Olympia’s garbage dump; the only thing that seemed to grow faster than the slums itself.
Cleaning and repairing a busted table or displaying damaged idol was much safer than eating the discarded food. After Olympia learned people were subsisting off “stolen” discarded scraps all garbage was coated in “divine repellent” (which in truth meant drenching them in hemlock) before being thrown out. It was that kind of strange cruelty from the big city that made the people of the slums resent them the most. But all the same they felt compelled to escape and join that caste above which abused them so.
Instead of focusing on the people in the city walls, within the slums neighbour turned on neighbour. Not that anyone had much to steal but desperation to get out of the slums and escape to the paradise just beyond the walls led many people down dark paths. Crime and abuse ran rampant with the only form of protection available ironically being from the Four Tyrants: the mob bosses who each dominated a quarter of the slums.
Within their turf, they offered protection… for a price. Those who didn’t pay were just as likely to be attacked by random criminals as they were agents of the spurned “protectors.” Whatever money one made in the slums was quickly funneled up to the Tyrants to keep their lavish lifestyles afloat. Unlike those under their thumbs, they liked the slums the way they were and had no interest in leaving. They determined it was better to be kings of a slum than average in high society.
In exchange for keeping the rowdy poor under control, the Four Tyrants were allowed to act unchecked and were even rumored to be directly working with the king of Olympia. They masked their illicit activities with the various legal fronts that their connections to the king and big city allowed.
As they jockeyed for power in various business squabbles and gang wars, the tyrants’ latest game was competing for share in the debt slave market. One tyrant realized that compared to taking what little the penniless had, it was more profitable to bankrupt and sell them as debt slaves to cults and big businesses.
It became increasingly common for people in the slums to sell their own children as collateral for their debts. After all, debt was passed down to one’s children after death and so it made little difference if they were sold now to give the parents a chance, or if they were enslaved alongside them.
“And that’s why they were after your sister?” Kat looked up from her tablet to ask.
“Yes. It is a good way to scare up money when times are tough around here. But it is also a threat they’d love to make good on. Child slaves are seen as a great investment for replenishing servants in the big city.” Kasious gravely replied.
“How awful.” Kat cried.
“Yeah. My parents always refused to sell us. But… they just worked themselves to death to try and make ends meet without doing so. I can’t let them take Glyka after all they’d sacrificed… but I don’t know how much longer I can keep her safe.” Kasious despaired. 
Glyka stopped playing with Kat’s pet and went to comfort her older brother.
“It’s even worse than was previously reported.” Kat went back to scribbling down notes.
The strange girl had asked to interview Kasious for a “report” of hers. In her debt, Kasious consented both to audio interview (whatever in the world that meant) as well as her taking notes. As Kasious talked she appeared to be writing something. Yet glancing at the metal tablet she wrote on revealed that she wasn’t writing anything at all! What a strange girl.
Then the water clock set for the baking process stopped running. Kasious checked to see the bread was ready.
“Ooh! Bread’s ready. Let’s take a pause and try it.” Kat tapped her earring for some reason.
As Kasious put the loaf on the table, he noticed Kat place the cost for a loaf of bread on the table: three drachmas and forty-nine obols. The drachma was originally equivalent to a worker’s daily wage but under seemingly endless inflation that benchmark had long since passed.
“You saved my sister, I couldn’t ask you to pay for my gift of gratitude. Especially since the grain quality is so poor; it isn’t worth an obol.” Kasious panicked.
“Let’s test it and see.” Kat cut the bread and tried a slice.
“…how is it?” Kasious asked.
“It’s not terrible!” Kat beamed.
“How reassuring…”
“No seriously, think about it. The grain is awful; the quality usually ground up for livestock meal. If the bread you made with it can taste this mediocre, you must be incredibly skilled.”
Kasious and Glyka stared at each other, utterly baffled by this response.
“Honestly Kasious, I wouldn’t pay money for this, and I work here.” Glyka told it how it was.

PART 2
Kasious hung his head in shame, “Darn. We’re ruined. There’s no way I’ll meet my protection fees this week. Maybe I should’ve just surrendered to the Mart when I had a chance.”
“The Mart?” Kat raised an eyebrow as she kept eating Kasious’ terrible bread, “You mean that big market up the road? They have a bakery?”
“They have everything and ever since they set up shop, they’ve been running all the local shops out of business. You saw them?”
“I tried to enter to write about them in my report, but they threw me out because of their strict no demi-humans allowed policy.” Kat pointed to her animal ears, “How are demi-human residents supposed to survive around here if they’re barred entry from vital locations?”
Kasious and Glyka looked uncomfortable in their Phrygian caps.
“But from what you described, it seems the Mart is as I expected.” Kat sighed.
“Their success defies belief. I’ve lowered our prices to basically be just above production cost yet the Mart is still always way lower, drawing away all our old customers. I don’t know how they’re making a profit with such low prices.” Kasious shook his head.
“They aren’t.” Kat finished another slice.
“What?”
“The Mart has a couple tricks at their disposal that you don’t. Given their size they can buy supply in bulk, which means they buy for less. Plus, as a subsidiary of the Cult of Demeter, they might be able to get all the grain they want at production or less from their parent organization. That already allows them to sell for less while making a profit. But if I had to guess, in order to achieve such drastic success, they are also intentionally using low bread prices as loss leaders. They have prices so low that they lose money on bread, but since they sell more than one thing, it is well worth it to get people in the door spending on other products. Even if they were still losing money, their war chest is supplied by the Cult of Demeter it’s fine to lose money for a bit.”
“But why would the cult of Demeter intentionally fund them if they were losing money?”
“They might lose money in the short term, but as they undercut small local businesses, they intend for all their competitors to die out. Once that happens, they can jack up their prices as much as they want and people will have no choice but to buy from them since all the alternate bakeries have been bought out or gone under.”
Kasious was horrified as he realized Kat had a point. Was that their play?
“The businesses here likely learned this tactic from the big city: in Olympia this is a common practice. If I had to guess, they’ve opened locations all across the slums in each of the Tyrants’ territory: giving them a cut in exchange for destroying the local economy.” Kat warned.
“If the Cult of Demeter and the tyrants are conspiring against us then… it really is over. We’re going to go under.” Kasious despaired.
“Not necessarily.”
“Nobody is going to pay for this.”
“I will.”
“I doubt you can eat enough bread to fund our entire business.”
“I won’t be eating it.”
Kasious and Glyka looked at Kat, wondering if they’d misheard her. 
“I know it sounds crazy, but I have an idea. If you trust me, I think I can help.” Kat grinned.

PART 3
In between the time Kasious directed Kat to the slums, and he reunited with her at the bakery, she had time to tour them and see their horrors firsthand. They aligned with what Kasious described: destitute people struggling for their next meal. Many showed early signs of malnourishment and starvation, having to ration what little they could afford.
That was why the line stretched around the block when Kasious’ Hellen Bakery announced it would be offering free bread as long as supply lasted. Upfront, Kat paid him enough for all the bread he could make from his supplies. Now it was just a matter of baking and serving whoever asked for it. Eventually the shop had become so busy he needed to constantly be baking to serve the lineup.
Kat had gone and returned with beggars she’d met in town: some bakers who’d recently lost everything. In exchange for taking home some of the bread they made, they would help Kasious in the kitchen with some extra ovens Kat and Glyka salvaged.
Kasious’ neighbours contributed by agreeing to let him use some of their space and chairs: helping set up a sitting area for people to wait for their orders and eat together. The unique, creative seating of the slums added vibrancy and aesthetics to the eating area. Beyond the average refurbished chairs, people also sat on broken column bases, shattered pottery made whole and flipped upside down, dismembered statues given new lives as chairs and couches.
Those who loaned their adapted furniture were surprised to be treated to free bread, thanks and compliments on their ingenuity. There were many whispers about the strange demi-human girl who helped organize this. Although, she dodged any focus on herself, instead promoting the man of the hour the local boy Kasious as well as everyone else who donated the time, labor and possessions to make this possible. After the initial setup, with the others’ help, it was a huge success: many of the poorest people sang Kasious’ praises.
But with the option of free bread, who would pay for it anymore? Kat’s plan to drum up business was simple: there was free regular bread but also paid bread which possibly contained some of the sacred grain Kasious received from the temple of Demeter. Those who bought a loaf containing some of that bread could win a prize: a voucher for free bread in the future. The gamble of a buy-one-get-one-free drew many interesting parties whose pay was added to what Kasious already received from Kat!
“Kasious… pinch me. Is this real life?” Glyka asked at the end of the day as she saw all the drachmas filling the empty container where the grain was meant to be.
“I can’t believe it either.” Kasious nearly teared up.
“We’re rich!” Glyka threw them in their ecstatically.
“No, we’re nearly out of debt.”
“Nearly!?”
“Yes, but another good day like this and we will actually be set.”
“Then you’re in luck. Tomorrow we restock on good grain and the real fun  begins..” Kat smiled as she joined the siblings. They rejoiced, amazed by this seeming miracle.

PART 4
That night, Kat asked if she could sleep in their supply shed for the night, much to Kasious’ confusion. Despite Kasious’ repeated offers to sleep there in her place and let her take his room, the stranger insisted.
Yet guilt ate away at the boy, especially when he heard it begin to rain. Kasious went out to the shack with a blanket for her. However, he was amazed to find Kat being kept warm by a great host of wild animals which cuddled around as she sat, writing nothing on that tablet as usual. The shed was illuminated by strange glowing markings on her clothing and gems in her jewelry. With her strange, wearable night-lights, she looked like an otherworldly spirit.
“Hiya Kasious. What’re you doing here so late?” Kat waved when she noticed him.
“I thought you needed a blanket to stay warm…” he awkwardly began.
“Nah, I’m all good. Check if Glyka needs it instead.”
“Right…”
Kasious prepared to leave but he paused as curiosity nagged at him. He turned around and had to ask.
“Lady Kat… are you a goddess in disguise?” Kasious trembled.
In Graia everyone grew up hearing tales of Theoxenia: stories of gods roaming the world in disguise, testing the moral qualities of mortals. If they were offered hospitality and respect even in disguise, they would reward their mortal hosts. If mistreated they would punish the insolent mortals accordingly. Was Kasious living through it now?
“It’s not lady Kat, silly. Just Kat. Olympus doesn’t consider me a goddess and neither do I, so there’s no need to fear me.” She said while petting the docile deer which slept next to her.
“I see. Well either way, thank you.” Kasious smiled.
“And thank you.” She smiled back, to his confusion.
“Me?”
“Yep. I originally came here just to write my report on Olympia. But when I saw things were even more dire than I feared, I despaired. Something has to be done and… I hope you’ll forgive me for using you as a means to that end.” Kat stood up and approached him.
“After seeing what you can do, I’d be happy to help. I owe you that much.” Kasious smiled.
“Thank you.” She put her hand on his shoulder, “And Kasious… sorry for not helping out with the baking itself earlier. Wearing human hats for long periods of time is unbearable for my demi-human ears, I wouldn’t wish that on my worst enemy. I dunno how you manage all day. In my homeland we use hairnets when baking instead. Maybe I’ll see about getting some for here.”
It was a strange comment, but one that made Kasious freeze up. But Kat just smiled sweetly at him and went back to writing nothing.
Kasious went back into his house, his heart pounding in his chest. He took off his hat and looked into the bronze mirror on his wall to see his reflection. His uncovered head showed off those accursed demihuman rat ears he’d learned to hide to survive in Graia.
Most ignorant Graiacs erroneously believed all demi-humans had one set of ears, so demi-humans trying to pass as human started to wear eye-catching earrings to draw attention to their human ears and distract from the headwear that covered their animal set of ears.
Of course she knew. Kasious smiled as he thought about it. Still, she treated him normally. Even demi-humans in Graia regarded each other as vermin but she… she accepted him anyways. With her in mind, the young baker stared at his reflection and smiled. For once he didn’t hate it.

Notes:
-	Mercantilism is the name of Graia/ Olympia’s economic system. In real life, Mercantilism was the economic epoch that preceded capitalism between the 16th and 18th centuries that was inextricably tied to colonialism and empire building. This fantasy Mercantilism is a mix of real-world Mercantilism, capitalism and the simpler ancient Greek economy. In the story it is hugely historically inaccurate and a clear sign that the gods (namely Hermes/ Mercury the god of trade) is meddling in mortal affairs to create a more complex economy than should exist.
-	The idea of the slums using broken and old stuff from Olympia was an idea I had to make their rundown city look like an ancient Greek ruin does to us today: everything broken and derelict. The difference being that the slums have actual people still living there: similar to real life with people living in the ruins of Rome after the empire’s collapse.
-	Hemlock was the most common poison in ancient Greece. If it sounds unreasonable that people in power would poison consumables to “punish criminals” just know that in the 1920s during prohibition when alcohol was supposed to be illegal, the U.S. government launched what it called the “Noble Experiment,” adding toxic chemicals like methanol and benzene to render industrial alcohol undrinkable. 
-	Instead of the fictional soaking of trash in hemlock in Olympia, in real life the US government poisoned ingredients used to produce bootleg, illegal alcohol in an attempt to stop its consumption. This intentional poisoning policy is estimated to have killed between 10,000 and 50,000 people. “According to a New York Times headline at the time, the government planned to “double alcohol poison content.” By the time Prohibition ended in 1933, more than 50,000 Americans had died from drinking tainted alcohol—a staggering 600 percent increase in alcohol-related deaths from previous years.” Read more: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/government-poison-alcohol-prohibition 
-	People sitting on column bases and dismembered statue heads is a reference to the famous painting of Pericles’ funeral Oration where some members of the audience are shown sitting on those things
-	Historically in ancient Rome grain doles were state-run programs which gave out heavily subsidized and eventually free grain or bread to about 200,000 of Rome's adult male citizens. Such populist policies were not really known in ancient Greece, even democratic Athens, didn’t have this measure.
-	Theoxenia is a real theme in Greek mythology as an extension of the Greek concept of “xenia” aka guest friendship or hospitality. Wikipedia says: Xenia was considered to be particularly important in ancient times when people thought that gods mingled among them; if one had poorly played host to a stranger, there was the risk of incurring the wrath of a god disguised as the stranger. Notable among them is the Greek god Zeus, who is sometimes called Zeus Xenios in his role as a protector of strangers. This normalized theoxeny or theoxenia, wherein human beings demonstrate their virtue by extending hospitality to a humble stranger (xenos), who turns out to be a disguised deity (theos).
-	Kasious’ animal ears being covered by ancient headwear is a reference to the myth of King Midas of Phrygia who was cursed with donkey ears by Apollo and so covered it up with a turban or other headwear.
-	Kasious specifically being a rat baker is a reference to the movie Ratatouille.
-	Kat Trivia: Kat learned baking from her grandma and is quite talented at it herself.
