*Myth ?.517: Toll

Glyka was excited to finally hang out with Kat again! Even though she was back from the Tyrant’s house, she was always so busy with others. Every other night she would be staying at someone else’s house working on some big grown-up stuff that went over Glyka’s head.
All she knew was that it made her day to hear Kat wanted to take Glyka out for a fun day in town. Doubly so when Glyka heard they weren’t touring the slums again, this time Kat was taking her to the big city: Olympia itself! Like everyone else in the slums, Glyka dreamed of nothing more than escaping to the paradise beyond the walls. But rumors of Kat doing that caused many to panic.
“Please… don’t leave us.” One of many in the crowd begged Kat not to leave them.
“Don’t worry, I’m just going to town to visit a friend who might be able to help us all. I promise to return before the day is done just like I always do.” Kat assured them all.
“Are you kidding! Let’s get outta here and live the good life! Never look back!” Glyka cackled adorably to Kasious’ embarrassment.
“She’s just joking. Obviously, I have to be back by her bedtime.” Kat giggled.
After waving to her friends in town, Kat brought Glyka to the dreaded toll booth. Nobody was allowed in Olympia’s walls without first paying the dreaded toll. These tollbooths were chokepoints for the slums; a cruel upwards wealth transfer on all those who needed to enter the city for work. The ever-rising tolls were so high that many had to work multiple jobs: one to pay for the toll and the rest for paying the Tyrants’ protection fees (with whatever remained going to survival).
The filthy troll of a man who ran the tollbooth was hated universally in the slums. He took such pleasure in the misery of the people he took money from that he was almost comically villainous. His appearance saw him compared to all sorts of awful creatures; the troll was hardly considered human by those for whom he served as a daily frustration. They knew he was doing his job, but why did he have to be so insufferable about it? 
None of them considered cruelty to be an unspoken part of the job. He was such a hateable piece of work that all the people’s anger was focused squarely on him rather than the investors who owned the actual tollbooth and were getting all the money at the end of the day. Similar to the use of the equally reviled private tax-collectors, a despicable middleman was an effective scapegoat and hatred sponge. But what made him so hateful?
In Olympia, the job of tollbooth operator was not only hated; it was seen as lowly. Those who occupied it were seen as failures who could not hold down any other form of employment, the worst of the worst. The tollbooth operator so insulted and diminished within a class-based society could only derive pleasure and satisfaction from kicking down at those they thought were inferior to them. That was why he was so rude and cruel to the slum dwellers: by making them less he thought he could feel better about himself. In a way it was quite sad.
Kat explained all this to a very confused Glyka. Glyka was just engaging in mocking mimicry towards the tolling troll after he finished being awful to the pair. She was hoping for Kat to join in the fun. She was not expecting the above lecture.
“You could’ve just laughed and moved on.” Glyka pouted.
“I thought it was a good opportunity to invite you to think.” Kat noted.
“I still don’t like him. Even Kasious hates him, and Kasious likes everyone!”
“I am not demanding you like him. I’m only asking you try understanding him.”
“You can only say that because the troll is nice to you. If I even get close to the booth when I’m out playing, he’ll start shouting and harassing me. But one look at a fancy dressed lady like you and he shuts up.”
“Perhaps. Though I’ve found his reaction to me more interesting than endearing. He seems to have notable conflict on his face about whether to treat me poorly as a demihuman and woman, or to let it slide when he sees my adornments.” Kat added.
“Must be nice being rich. Nobody calls you a dirty demi when you have money…” Glyka felt her Phrygian cap, feeling ashamed about the demi-human ears she hid under them.
“Glyka, I made you a promise. I intend to keep it.” Kat reassured her, giving the little girl pause.
Before long, the pair finished passing through the agricultural lands between walls and had arrived at the inner wall to the city proper. The toll booth here was operated by a simple automated device rather than any man. Perhaps it was a show of the tollbooth owner’s wealth to display this kind of mechanism which was all the rage among the elite. Or perhaps it was to further put the human tollbooth operators “in their place” and remind them they were worth the minimal amounts the were paid. After all, they were replaceable.
The automated tollbooth designed after the famous holy-water vending machines used in temples accepted coins and once the weight was enough, it unlocked the gate to the city for a set time before resetting. Quite the cutting-edge technology, befitting the entrance to the capital of Graiac civilization.
A statue of Angelos, the Goddess of doorways, city walls and borders fittingly stood atop the machine. Fitting the Graiac standards of beauty, she was curvy and pear-shaped; a sign of a wealthy well-maintained woman. For some reason, Kat spent a while inspecting the goddess’ depiction with visible amusement.
“Do you think that goddess looks anything like this in real life?” Glyka asked.
“Not at all. They really do just make every goddess look like Venus, don’t they.” Kat laughed, “I wonder if she’s seen this.”
After passing through the final gate, they arrived in Olympia! Glyka was so excited: since the tolls were too expensive, she had never seen the city before. This was the paradise the people of the slums all dreamed of living in? It barely looked any better than the slums outside the walls!
“The slums within Olympia aren’t much better than the slums without. The Tyrants’ influence is very diminished… but the dominion of the King does not treat them much better.” Kat snapped the disillusioned Glyka out of it.
“Why… why would anyone dream of escaping to this?” Glyka cried.
“Because Olympia has sold everyone a dream that they won’t end up here. The real dream of living in Olympia is there: near the mountain.” Kat pointed to Mount Olympus itself.
As she said, the closer the city got to the sacred mountain the richer and more amazing it became. Glyka was shocked. She thought the walls of Olympia were to keep poverty out. But in the city, one could see the visible transition from rich to poor. In the shadow of great abundance was great destitution.
“This is what the tollbooth operator probably returns to every day. Do you understand now why I pity him?” Kat asked.
Once Kat and Glyka arrived in Olympia, the first order of business was delivering things for people who couldn’t afford to go into town that day. Kat carried a comically massive bag behind her and carried out the deliveries. It was a literal chore, but Kat kept things fun by asking Glyka what differences she noticed from the slums beyond the walls.
Glyka noticed that the markets seemed different. The slums beyond the walls had several small markets; collections of little local businesses because they were not built up enough for a single, central market in the agora city center. The poorer parts of Olympia that were barely inside the walls meanwhile had multiple small markets as well, but they looked like colonized land. Kat took note of Glyka’s analogy to military conquest.
At every poor market they went through, the same signs and labels were visible above the stalls. Rather than any local shop proudly waving their own banner, some larger business had bought them all up, planting their emblem as proof of their conquest. Glyka, who had no formal education, couldn’t even read the store names that well, but she could still identify them by their logos: offensive to the eyes. Even outside the shops, as far as the eye could see there was signage and graffiti desperate to remind you that the shop was nearby; they were inescapable.
The natural world had been paved over completely. No grassy fields or forests as one might expect from beyond the walls. And yet after destroying nature they forgot to put anything else. The poor parts of Olympia were dusty dirt roads; in that regard they’d lost something the outer slums took for granted and seemingly gained nothing.
At least many of them they had horses and chariots; status symbols that were a necessity to traverse the gigantic city of Olympia’s roadways. People beyond the walls could only dream of such transportation but seeing it up close, she didn’t understand why. Their houses languished as she watched many of the men spending time and money maintaining their horse and chariot. Naturally there were various shops under the same banner offering aid with maintain their transportation, for a price.
“Do you know whose symbol that is?” Kat asked, noting the recurring brand Glyka kept pointing to.
“Um… feetius, god of sandals?” Glyka shrugged.
“Not exactly.” Kat laughed, “Those are the winged sandals of Hermes, god of speed and travel.”
Glyka was supposed to be the one explaining, but Kat always had information to give in return. Yet in the end, Kat still thanked Glyka for her observations, noting she didn’t even notice some of them. With the girl’s permission she would include them in her report. However, after running errands for people beyond town, Kat said she had to meet with someone in a certain temple. She left Glyka outside to wait with the kitten Gatola serving as her guard. However, while Kat was in a meeting, Glyka got bored of waiting and wandered towards a crowd that was gathering.
 
Notes:
-	The toll booth section is based on my experience taking a trip to the United States of America and being utterly SHOCKED at how many toll booths were everywhere. As a Canadian this was utterly foreign to me. Furthermore, I began to notice American comedians using “toll booth operator” as a demeaning insult for an unskilled person.
-	The automatic tollbooth machine that accepts coins may seem out of place in ancient times, but is actually a reference to the 2000-year-old vending machine used to dispense temple holy water. It was invented by the Greek engineer Heron of Alexandria to ensure that everyone got the same amount of holy water automatically (to stop people taking too much).
